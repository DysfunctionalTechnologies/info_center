Solar power is one of the fastest-changing energy technologies on Earth.
The basic idea is simple: photovoltaic (PV) cells convert photons into
electricity. What’s not simple is how far the industry has come in a
decade.

Where things stand now
The world passed 3 terawatts of installed solar capacity in early 2026.
It took decades to reach the first terawatt and only a few years to
triple that. In 2025 the industry added on the order of 600+ GW in a
single year (estimates vary by source, commonly cited around 510–664 GW).
Solar now supplies about 9–10% of global electricity, and on an average
day in the first half of 2026 it met over a quarter of world demand at
midday. 

ember-energy.org

China still dominates both manufacturing and new installations (often
more than half of annual additions). India, the US, the EU, and Brazil
are the next large markets. 2026 is expected to be the first year in two
decades with a modest drop in new installations—mostly because of policy
changes in China—not because solar got worse.

Why it exploded
- Costs collapsed. Utility-scale solar’s global average LCOE has been
  around $44/MWh recently, after falling ~90% since 2010. In many sunny
  regions, new solar is cheaper than new coal or gas, and in some cases
  cheaper than running existing coal plants. Commercial silicon modules
  now routinely exceed 24% efficiency. Lab perovskite–silicon tandem
  cells have reached ~35.5%. 

irena.org

The real constraint is no longer “is it cheap?” - It’s integration:
Solar is a midday resource. Without storage or flexible demand, evenings
still belong to other generators. Battery costs have also fallen
dramatically (global averages around $140/kWh installed by 2025). 2026
battery additions are large enough that they could shift a much bigger
share of new solar into evening hours. Grids, interconnection queues,
silver in cells, land use, recycling of old panels, and manufacturing
overcapacity are the live issues.

Two main families of solar:
PV (almost everything being built): silicon, increasingly TOPCon /
n-type, plus emerging tandems.

Concentrated solar power (CSP): mirrors + heat, useful where you want
thermal storage, but far smaller in new capacity.

Do not strap panels flat to the ridges. Rails that hold the glass 80–150 mm off the tin let air wash the hot backs of the panels and the roof. That gap is both PV cooling and house cooling. Tell the installer “ventilated rack, not flush.”2. Cover the second-floor roof first

This matters as much as the panels for a tin + hardiflex sandwich:Double-sided foil / “sisalation” under the purlins, shiny side toward the air gap  
Or foil-faced foam boards between beams, taped at joints  
Seal obvious gaps so hot air does not pour into the room

Foil stops radiant heat from the glowing tin. Hardiflex alone barely does.4. Vent the cavity
A high vent at the ridge and low vents at the eaves (insect screen) so the space between tin and hardiflex can exhale. Without that, you just built a foil-lined oven.5. Ceiling fan + the 1 HP AC you already have

Stow-to-flat is mandatory. The failure mode is not “it doesn’t track.” It is “anemometer dies, actuator jams, or power is out, and you have a 9 kW sail on tin.” A locked flat array survives. A half-raised array peels L-feet and the roof.Rain for weeks
Pivots, screws, linear actuators, and limit switches live in habagat. They corrode, bind, and eat limit switches. Residential trackers are a service business. You would become that business.Structure
A pivot at the ridge and a lift at the eave puts moving point loads into purlins that were designed for dead load + wind, not a daily hinge. The second-floor tin you want to cool wants panels hugging the sheet with a small air gap — not a kinetic sculpture.Wires and warranties
Daily flex on PV cable. Module frames not certified for that mount. Inverter still sees the same 8–10 kWp; you did not gain service-panel headroom.Gain vs cost
A clean single-axis tracker on open ground might add ~15–25% energy.
